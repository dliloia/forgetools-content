Urgency is a tool. Some leaders use it like a hammer for everything.

When every message is a five-alarm fire, your team stops treating fires as emergencies. They've seen too many skies fall and un-fall. So they get good at looking busy without fully committing, because committing to the new priority means abandoning the last real thing they were working on. That's not laziness. That's rational adaptation to an unreliable signal.

The cycle-time cost is real and almost nobody tracks it. A single "DROP EVERYTHING" moment across a five-person team burns over three hours of capacity when you account for context-switching on both ends. Run that twice a week and you've got hundreds of lost person-hours a year, none of which shows up as a leadership problem on any dashboard.

The harder cost is what happens to your credibility over time. Eventually something will actually be on fire. And your team will apply the same skeptical filter they've been building for months. You'll have spent the signal when you needed it most.
