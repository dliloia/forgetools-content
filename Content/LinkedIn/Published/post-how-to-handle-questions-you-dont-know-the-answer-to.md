Faking an answer in a Q&A is almost always visible to the room.

The instinct makes sense. You've just spent time building credibility with an audience, and the last thing you want is a question that exposes a gap. So you start talking before you know where you're going, and you produce something that sounds like an answer without being one. The audience can usually tell, and that's where the real credibility loss happens.

The stronger move is to say "I don't know" like a statement, because it is one. No apology, no performance of humility. Just the fact, followed by a clear next step: you'll follow up, you'll redirect to something genuinely related, or you'll open it to the room. Any of those works. Rambling doesn't.

What most people miss is that you can prepare for this even though you can't predict every question. Think through the ones you don't want to get, then decide in advance how you'll handle them. That decision, made before you're under pressure, is where the composure comes from.

If you manage a team, this matters beyond your own presentations. The way you handle not knowing is a signal about what your organization values. One clean "I don't know" from a leader does more for psychological safety than most deliberate culture initiatives.
