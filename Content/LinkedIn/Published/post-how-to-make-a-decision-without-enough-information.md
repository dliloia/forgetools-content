Most teams don't stall because they lack data. They stall because they're afraid.

I've seen this pattern more times than I can count. A decision sits open for weeks, and everyone keeps asking for more information, more analysis, more stakeholder input. But the data was never really the issue. The issue was that making a call means owning the outcome, and that's uncomfortable.

The practical cost of that discomfort adds up fast. Candidates take other offers. Competitors ship first. Team members read the indecision as a signal about the culture and start looking elsewhere. Delay feels safe because its costs are diffuse and delayed. They're still real.

Two questions cut through most of this: what does waiting actually cost, and how reversible is being wrong? Answer both honestly and the path forward usually becomes obvious. Most decisions are far more reversible than we treat them, and most delays are far more costly than we admit.

The managers who get this right aren't smarter or better informed. They've just made peace with uncertainty as a permanent condition of the job, and they've learned to move thoughtfully anyway.
