# How Empathy Helps Managers Make Better Decisions

Empathy helps a manager understand what an employee is experiencing before deciding what to do. It does not require agreeing with every request or lowering expectations. It means getting enough context to respond fairly.

A missed deadline, a drop in participation, or a change in quality can have more than one explanation. Ask before you decide what the behavior means.

## Ask questions that get past status

"How is the project going?" can produce a quick "fine." Try a question about the work: "What is slowing this down?" or "Which part of this is taking more effort than expected?"

When you notice a change, describe what you observed without assigning a motive. "I noticed the last two updates came in late" is easier to discuss than "You seem disengaged."

Then listen to the answer. Do not jump straight to advice. Ask what the person has tried and what would help. They may need a decision, clearer priorities, a resource, or a chance to explain a constraint.

## Separate understanding from agreement

You can understand why someone is struggling and still hold a clear standard. Empathy gives you more information before you decide how to respond.

If expectations were unclear, clarify them. If workload is the issue, review priorities.

For example, if a teammate has missed two updates, you might say: "I noticed the last two updates came in after the deadline. Is something getting in the way? The team needs the update by Thursday so the next handoff can happen. Let's work out what will make that possible." This names the behavior and the work requirement while leaving room to learn what is happening. If the person needs support or an accommodation, follow your organization's process. If the performance gap remains after the facts are clear, address it directly.

Avoid making assumptions about personal circumstances. Offer room to share, but do not pressure someone to disclose private information.

## Remember what matters to the person

In a one-on-one, employees may share goals, constraints, or concerns that affect their work. Keep appropriate notes and return to the topic later. If you said you would check on something, follow through.

Store personal information carefully and only where your organization permits. A manager does not need to record every detail of someone's life to remember an agreed next step.

## Practice empathy without turning it into a script

Use a few reliable habits: ask before interpreting, listen for the answer, check your understanding, and state what will happen next. Sometimes the answer is a change in support. Sometimes it is a clear performance conversation. Both can be respectful.

Empathy also has limits. You cannot promise that every situation can be accommodated or solved. Be honest about what you can change, what needs another decision-maker, and when you will follow up.

## Start in the next one-on-one

Choose one question that goes beyond status. Give the employee time to answer. Summarize what you heard and ask if you understood it correctly.

That small exchange can reveal information you need to manage well. Use it to make a better decision, then keep your expectations and commitments clear.
