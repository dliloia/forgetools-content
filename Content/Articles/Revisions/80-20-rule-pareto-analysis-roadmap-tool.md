# How to Use the 80/20 Rule to Prioritize a Roadmap

The 80/20 rule is useful when you treat it as a question to investigate, not a promise that exactly 80 percent of results come from exactly 20 percent of causes. Look at the problems your team tracks, group them by cause, and find which ones create the most cost or pain. That list can help shape a roadmap.

## Start with evidence you already have

Choose a set of problems your team can review: support requests, defects, missed deadlines, service interruptions, customer complaints, or rework. Use a defined time period and include enough detail to understand what happened.

If there is no reliable record, start by creating one. A Pareto chart cannot recover information that was never collected. Do not fill gaps with guesses and present them as measurements.

## Group problems by cause

A list of individual incidents can hide the pattern. Five reports about the same confusing export workflow may be five incidents, but one underlying problem.

Ask people close to the work to review the categories. Similar symptoms can have different causes, and different symptoms can trace back to the same cause. The grouping is a judgment call, so write down how you made it.

## Compare frequency with impact

Count how often each category appears. Then estimate the cost or consequence of each occurrence using a measure that fits the decision. That might be time lost, severity, customer impact, or money. Keep the raw count and the impact estimate visible.

A frequent low-impact problem may deserve a quick fix because it affects many people. A rare but severe failure may deserve attention because the cost of each incident is high. Ranking by count alone can miss the second case. Ranking by severity alone can miss widespread friction.

Use more than one view before choosing the work. If estimates are uncertain, label them and revisit them when better data is available.

For example, imagine a team has 40 reports of a confusing settings page and one report of a failed data export that delayed a customer deliverable. The settings issue may lead by frequency, while the export failure may lead by severity. Review both before deciding. If the export failure could recur, investigate it even if the count is low. If the settings issue is easy to fix and wastes time for many users, it may be the better first task. The ranking informs the choice, it does not make it for you.

## Turn the ranking into a decision

Sort the categories by the measure that matches the outcome you want to improve. Review the highest-ranked items with the team and test the assumptions. Are the categories sound? Does the impact estimate reflect what users experience? Is the cause within your control?

The top of the list can inform the next planning cycle. It should not automatically become the roadmap. Check strategic commitments, dependencies, risks, and the needs of groups that appear less often in the data. A small customer segment can still matter.

For each candidate, write down the problem, its evidence, the proposed change, and how you will tell whether the change helped. Assign an owner and a review date.

## Keep the long tail visible

The 80/20 principle can become an excuse to ignore everything outside the top few items. Do not let that happen. Some lower-frequency problems may create accessibility barriers, security risks, or disproportionate harm for a particular group. They need a separate review even if they rank low on volume.

A monthly review can help keep the list current. Recheck the categories, look for changes in frequency or impact, and close out problems that no longer appear. The first review may take time. Later reviews should be shorter if the team has kept its records usable.

The value is not in repeating a familiar ratio. It is in replacing the loudest-request-wins conversation with a clearer account of what is causing trouble and what the team can reasonably address next.
