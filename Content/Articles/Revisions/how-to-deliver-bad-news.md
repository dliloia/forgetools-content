# How to Deliver Bad News Clearly and Respectfully

When you have to deliver bad news, say the decision or concern plainly, then pause. A long preamble can leave the person guessing while you try to make the conversation easier for yourself.

Preparation matters because the person needs clear information, a chance to respond, and an accurate account of what happens next.

## Know what you can say

Before the meeting, confirm the decision, the reason you are authorized to share, and the next steps. For a layoff or other employment action, coordinate with HR and follow your organization's process. Confirm logistics, timing, benefits information, documents, and the right contact for questions. Do not guess at answers.

For a performance conversation, review the specific examples, expectations, support available, and any formal process that applies. Make sure the employee has heard relevant feedback before this meeting. A surprise warning is often a sign that earlier conversations did not happen clearly enough.

For example, instead of "Your communication needs work," name the work expectation and the observed gap: "The project update was due Friday, and I did not receive it. The next update is due Friday at noon. What is getting in the way of meeting that?" Use this only when it matches the actual standard and the employee has had a fair chance to understand it.

Write the first sentence and practice saying it out loud. Keep it direct and factual.

## Lead with the news

Use language the person can understand. For example: "Your role is being eliminated as part of the restructuring." Or: "We need to discuss a repeated missed deadline and agree on what must change."

Then stop. Give the person a moment to process before you explain details. Do not bury the decision under appreciation, background, or vague phrases such as "we are exploring options" when the decision is already final.

## Separate different kinds of conversations

A layoff and a performance concern are different situations. Do not imply that a business decision reflects the employee's performance if it does not. If performance is the concern, give concrete examples and explain the expectations and consequences according to company policy.

Avoid making promises you cannot control. If the person asks about timing, compensation, or an appeal, share only confirmed information and direct them to the right contact.

## Make room for the reaction

People may become quiet, angry, tearful, or focused on practical questions. Listen without arguing with the reaction or trying to talk the person into feeling better.

You can say, "Take a moment. I'm here when you're ready." If they ask a question you cannot answer, tell them you will find out and give a specific time for follow-up. Do not say "everything will be fine" when you cannot know that.

## End with a clear next step

Summarize what happens after the meeting, who owns each action, and when the person will hear from you. For a performance plan, schedule check-ins and provide the support you committed to. For a role change, share the confirmed logistics and contact information.

Follow up when you said you would. Do not make the employee chase you for information that was part of the conversation.

Difficult conversations are part of management. Clear language does not remove the impact, but it does reduce confusion. Prepare the facts, say what is happening, listen, and follow through.
