# Three Eagle Scout Principles Managers Can Use at Work

Three principles from my Eagle Scout experience still help me think about management: be prepared, serve because it matters, and leave things better than you found them.

They sound simple. The work is in applying them before a problem forces your hand.

## Be prepared

Preparation is not predicting every problem. It is thinking ahead about likely risks and keeping useful information where you can find it.

Regular one-on-ones can help you hear about friction before it becomes urgent. Notes on goals, feedback, and commitments keep you from reconstructing a year of work during review season. In hiring, knowing what a role requires and what evidence you want can make an interview more focused.

These habits do not guarantee you will catch every issue. They give you a better chance to notice change early and make decisions from evidence rather than memory.

## Serve because the work matters

Management is not a prize for being the strongest individual contributor. Your job is to help people do their work, grow, and understand what is expected.

That includes direct conversations. Avoiding necessary feedback may feel kind, but it leaves the employee without information they need. Service means giving people the truth with respect and support.

## Leave the team stronger

Think about what would happen if you were out for a month or moved to another role. Could someone find the key decisions, continue the work, and know whom to contact?

Document the process that matters. Share context instead of keeping every decision in your head. Develop people who can take on more responsibility. You do not have to make yourself unnecessary overnight. Avoid building a team that cannot move without you.

Before a difficult week or new project, ask: What should I prepare now? What would help the people doing the work? What should be stronger when this is done?

I earned my Eagle Scout rank as a teenager. These principles have stayed useful because they are plain enough to remember and demanding enough to practice. Prepare before the deadline. Serve the people, not your ego. Leave the team with more knowledge and options than it had before.
