# Why Managers Need to Document and Teach the Work

If a new employee has to ask you the same process questions every week, the answer probably belongs somewhere the team can find it. Training and documentation are part of managing the work.

"Ask me when you need help" sounds supportive, but it can leave a new person waiting for your availability. It also makes the team depend on one person for routine answers.

## Start with the questions people repeat

You do not need to write a massive playbook before improving onboarding. Start with the tasks a new employee must do in their first few weeks.

Write down how to complete the task, where to find the tools or forms, who approves exceptions, and what to do when something goes wrong. If a short screen recording is clearer than a written guide, use one. Keep the instructions in a shared place with a name people can search.

The first version can be brief. Ask someone who did not write it to follow the steps and point out what is missing.

## Capture the work while you do it

When you explain a recurring process, jot down the steps before the details fade. Include decisions that require judgment, not just button clicks. For example, note what information to check before escalating a customer issue and which cases need a manager's review.

Do not record passwords, confidential employee details, or customer information in a general guide. Keep access aligned with the people who need it and follow your organization's data rules.

Review instructions when the process changes. Add an owner and a date for the next review, especially for work that affects money, customers, safety, or compliance.

## Check whether the team can work without you

Imagine you are away for a few days. Can someone handle routine work using the documentation and the team's normal escalation path? If not, identify the gap.

This is not a test of whether another person can replace your judgment. Some decisions require experience and authority. The check is whether predictable work can continue without waiting for you to repeat the same instructions.

Use the same approach when someone changes roles or leaves. Capture the recurring decisions, dependencies, and open work while that person is available to explain them.

## Teach the reasoning, not only the steps

A guide cannot cover every exception. Explain why the process works the way it does and where the employee should pause to ask for help. Pair the person with a knowledgeable teammate for practice, then let them do the task while you observe.

Training is not finished when you send a link. Ask the employee to walk through the task, answer questions, and show where they would look if they got stuck. Update the guide based on what they missed.

For example, a hypothetical refund guide could show where to find the order, which cases need approval, how to record the decision, and where to escalate an exception. A new teammate can test the guide with a practice case before handling a real one. If they cannot tell which cases need approval, clarify that rule before calling the guide complete.

## Make knowledge easy to maintain

Assign an owner for each important guide. Give teammates a simple way to flag errors or request an update. If the process has no owner, the guide will decay as soon as the work changes.

Pick one question your team asks repeatedly. Document the answer, show the team where it lives, and revise it after someone tries it. That small habit makes work easier to learn and less dependent on memory.
