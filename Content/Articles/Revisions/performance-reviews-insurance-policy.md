Proposed title: How to Write a Better Performance Review From Your 1:1 Notes
Excerpt: A useful performance review starts before review season. Capture work, feedback, commitments, and outcomes in your regular 1:1 notes, then use those records to write a review with specific examples.

## Start with evidence, not memory

When review season opens, it is tempting to start with a blank document and search your memory for the past six months. That is a bad place to begin. Recent work comes to mind first. A project from February does not.

The result is a review full of broad praise: "strong communicator," "reliable teammate," "meets expectations." Those phrases may be true, but they do not tell the employee what they did, what changed because of it, or what to do next.

A better performance review is a summary of evidence you have collected during the year. Your one-on-ones give you a regular place to collect it.

## Take notes while the work is fresh

You do not need a long transcript after every meeting. Capture a few facts while you still remember the conversation. What work did the employee move forward? What did they commit to? What feedback did you discuss? What happened after that?

Keep the notes close to the one-on-one record, and date each entry. Separate what you observed from what you inferred. "The launch checklist was complete two days before the deadline" is an observation. "They are highly organized" is a conclusion. The conclusion may be fair, but the observation gives you something to support it.

When feedback comes up, record the example and the follow-up. If you asked someone to send a shorter project update and their next update was easier for the team to use, write that down. A month later, you will not have to reconstruct whether the feedback landed.

## Turn a note into useful review evidence

A note is raw material. The review connects it to the expectations for the role.

Suppose your note says, "Rebuilt the onboarding checklist after two new hires missed the same setup step." That is more useful than "takes initiative." In the review, explain what happened, what the employee did, and why it mattered to the team. Then connect that example to the relevant expectation, such as improving a shared process or helping colleagues become productive.

Do not inflate a routine task into a major accomplishment. Give the work its proper weight. Specificity is more credible than praise that grows each time you retell it.

For a performance area that needs work, use the same standard. Name the behavior, the effect it had, and what needs to change. If you already gave that feedback during the year, include the follow-up you observed. A review should not introduce a serious concern the employee has never heard before.

## Use the record to check your own judgment

Notes can help you notice when one recent event is taking over your view of the whole year. Read entries from different parts of the review period. Look for patterns, and check whether the examples fit the expectations you are evaluating.

The record will not make your judgment automatic. You still need to decide which work matters and how it compares with the role. It does make those decisions easier to explain. If your conclusion depends on a single moment, say so and give the employee the context. If several examples point in the same direction, use them.

Also compare the amount of detail across people. If one employee has several pages of notes and another has two lines, that may reflect a difference in work, but it may also reflect a difference in what you noticed or documented. Do not let the size of the file decide the rating.

## If you are already behind

You can still write a thoughtful review without a full year of notes. Start with the records you do have: project plans, delivered work, customer feedback, decisions, and earlier one-on-one notes. Ask the employee to help reconstruct the year. They often remember work you did not see directly.

Mark reconstructed details as such. Do not write an inference as if you observed it. If you cannot verify a claim, leave it out or ask a follow-up question before using it.

Then make the next one-on-one the start of a better record. After the meeting, write down the important work, the commitments, and the follow-up date. You are not going to recreate a perfect year of documentation in one afternoon. You can stop the same blank-page problem from returning.

## A simple note habit

After each one-on-one, spend a few minutes recording the work discussed, feedback given, commitments made, and anything that needs follow-up. Keep the wording factual. Return to the notes at the next meeting so they help the employee, not only the annual review.

That routine gives you useful material for development conversations throughout the year. It also makes review season easier. You are organizing work you already discussed instead of trying to remember a year in one sitting.
