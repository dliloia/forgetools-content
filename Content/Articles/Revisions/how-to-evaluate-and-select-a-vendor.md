# How to Evaluate and Select a Vendor

To choose a vendor well, decide what your organization needs before you watch a demo. Otherwise the most polished presentation can quietly become the requirements list.

A useful evaluation starts with the work the vendor must support, the limits you have to meet, and the people who will use the service.

## Write requirements before contacting vendors

Describe the problem in plain language. What needs to work? What happens if the new service fails? What systems does it need to connect to? Who will use it, and what volume or workload should it handle?

Separate requirements into must-haves and preferences. Must-haves should be real disqualifiers. If everything is a must-have, the list will not help you choose.

Include security, privacy, accessibility, support, implementation, and exit requirements where they apply. Ask the people who own those areas to review the list before you send it out. Requirements depend on your organization, so do not copy a generic checklist without adapting it.

## Set a scoring method in advance

Choose the categories that matter and assign a weight to each one before proposals arrive. Then use the same rating scale for each vendor and record the evidence behind each score.

A weighted score can structure a discussion, but it should not make the decision by itself. Some requirements are pass or fail. A high total should not erase a serious gap in a must-have. Treat a failed must-have as a stop or exception that requires a named approver, a written rationale, and a plan to reduce the risk. Do not let strong optional features quietly compensate for a requirement the organization said it could not compromise.

Keep the rubric fixed during the comparison. If a requirement changes, write down why and rescore every vendor consistently.

For example, a hypothetical team choosing a scheduling service might treat single sign-on and data export as must-haves, while treating custom report colors as a preference. A vendor that cannot meet a must-have should not win only because it scored well on optional features. If the team is considering an exception, document who approved it and how the gap will be managed.

## Ask for evidence in the proposal

Give vendors the same questions and the same context. Ask for specific answers about implementation, responsibilities, support coverage, data handling, integrations, service limits, and ongoing costs.

Avoid questions that invite generic marketing language. Instead of asking vendors to "describe onboarding," ask what your team must provide, what stages the work includes, and what could delay it.

Ask for references from customers with similar needs. Speak with them directly if possible. Ask what worked, what took longer than expected, and what they wish they had clarified before signing.

## Run a demo using your scenarios

Send each vendor a few realistic scenarios in advance. Ask them to show how the product or service would handle the work your team actually does.

Invite the people who will use it day to day. They may notice steps or limitations that executives and evaluators miss. Watch how the vendor handles a question they cannot answer or a workflow that does not behave as expected.

Record what you observed and compare it with the requirements, not with how entertaining the presentation was.

## Compare the full cost and risk

Look beyond the initial quote. Include implementation, training, support, usage fees, renewal terms, required add-ons, and the cost of leaving. Build a comparison across the period you expect to use the service, and label estimates clearly.

Review contract terms with the appropriate people in your organization. Confirm who owns the data, how it can be exported, and what happens at termination. Do not rely on verbal assurances for obligations that should be in writing.

## Make and document the decision

Review scores, evidence, risks, and unresolved questions together. If the numbers are close, use judgment, but explain the tradeoff and why it is acceptable.

Record the selected vendor, the alternatives considered, the reason for the decision, and the measures you will use to review the relationship after launch. A disciplined process makes the choice easier to explain and gives you a baseline if the service does not meet expectations.
