# How to Give Feedback to Someone More Senior Than You

Giving feedback to a senior leader is harder when your work or career depends on them. You do not need to pretend that power difference is harmless. You do need to decide whether the issue matters, what evidence you have, and whether this is a safe setting to raise it.

## Decide whether to raise it

Start with the impact. Is the behavior affecting the team, the work, or an important decision? If it is a personal irritation with no meaningful effect, it may not need a formal conversation. If it is creating repeated confusion or harm, silence has a cost too.

Think about the risk in your specific workplace. Some leaders respond well to candid input. Others have shown that disagreement can bring retaliation. If you have reason to fear that, consider a trusted mentor, HR partner, or another appropriate channel before approaching the person directly. Your safety and livelihood matter.

## Prepare a clear example

Choose one or two specific moments. Describe what happened, what effect you observed, and what you are asking the leader to consider. Avoid guessing at motive. As a hypothetical script, you could say, "In the last two project reviews, the team did not get time to raise delivery risks before the decision was final. We missed a dependency that changed the schedule. Could we reserve time for risks before closing the decision?"

That is more useful than a broad claim about someone's leadership style. It gives them a concrete event and a possible next step. If your evidence comes from others, do not present private conversations as a group consensus unless those people have agreed.

## Choose the setting and say the point early

A private conversation is usually easier for both people than feedback in front of a group. Ask for time and explain the topic without making it sound like a surprise attack. Then state the observation directly, give the context, and pause to hear their perspective.

The goal is not to win an argument or prove that the leader is a bad person. The goal is to share information they may not have and discuss what could change. Stay open to details you did not know.

## Pay attention to the response

A senior person may need time to think. Agree on whether you will follow up and when. You are responsible for raising the concern clearly. You cannot control whether they accept it.

If a direct conversation is not a safe or effective option, do not treat courage as the only answer. Consider whether the issue belongs with another leader, HR, an ethics channel, or a documented process. Choose the route that fits the seriousness of the concern and the protections available to you.

If they punish you for a respectful, work related concern, treat that response as information about the environment. Keep a factual record and seek support through the channels available to you.

Upward feedback can help a team make better decisions, but it is not always risk free. Use judgment, ground your point in observable examples, and choose the safest effective path.
