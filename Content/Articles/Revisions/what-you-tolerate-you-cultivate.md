# What You Tolerate, You Cultivate: How Managers Set Team Standards

When a manager repeatedly ignores a behavior, the team can reasonably assume it is acceptable. That is the practical meaning of "what you tolerate, you cultivate." Standards come from what leaders address, not only from what they announce.

This does not mean every missed deadline or awkward comment deserves a formal correction. Managers need judgment. A person may need support, a deadline may have changed, or the behavior may be a one time mistake. The problem is a pattern that goes unspoken until everyone has built routines around it.

## Notice when an exception becomes the norm

One missed commitment can be a useful conversation. Several missed commitments without any discussion teach the team that the date is flexible. A dismissive comment in a meeting may be a poor moment. Repeated dismissive behavior that goes unaddressed can make people stop contributing.

Look for the workarounds your team has normalized. Do people avoid asking a certain colleague for input? Has a broken handoff become an unofficial process? Are concerns only discussed privately because raising them in a meeting feels pointless? These patterns may tell you that a standard is not being applied consistently.

Ask what is causing the behavior before deciding what it means. You may discover unclear ownership, an unrealistic workload, or a skill gap. Accountability is more useful when it addresses the cause as well as the effect.

## Separate patience from avoidance

Patience gives someone time and support to improve. Avoidance leaves the person guessing. If you have not explained the concern, the expected behavior, or how you will follow up, the employee may not know there is a problem.

Have the conversation privately and use a specific example. Explain the impact, ask for their perspective, and agree on what should happen next time. If there is a legitimate constraint, adjust the plan. If the person needs practice, provide it and check progress.

For example, if a teammate has missed two agreed handoffs, explain how the delay affected the next team and ask what got in the way. Agree on the next handoff date and how they will flag a risk early. Then follow up on that agreement instead of waiting for another miss.

## Reinforce the behavior you want

Standards are not only about correction. When someone raises a risk early, follows through on a commitment, or challenges an idea respectfully, name the action and why it helped. That tells the team what good work looks like in practice.

Be consistent across people. If the same behavior gets excused for one person and criticized in another, the team will notice. Apply expectations fairly, and revisit them when the situation changes.

## Close the gap this week

Pick one recurring issue you have left unaddressed. Check your assumptions, prepare a concrete example, and talk with the person involved. A clear conversation now is usually easier than correcting a team norm after it has spread.

Your team learns from what you let pass and what you reinforce. Make those choices deliberately.
