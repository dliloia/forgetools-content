# How to Give Feedback That Leads to a Useful Conversation

Feedback works best when the person can connect it to a real moment and understand why it matters. Save a concern for months and it becomes a surprise. Keep it vague and the employee has nothing concrete to change.

## Talk about behavior while the example is fresh

If someone interrupts a colleague in a meeting, address that behavior soon, in private. Describe what you observed and the effect it had. Do not turn one moment into a judgment about their character.

A simple structure can help you think: situation, behavior, impact. In a hypothetical planning meeting, you might say, "Yesterday, you spoke over Maya twice while she was explaining the delivery risk. We moved on before the team understood the dependency." This gives the person something specific to respond to. It also lets them correct your account if you missed context.

The structure is a prompt, not a script. If you cannot name a specific moment, pause and ask whether you have enough evidence to give the feedback. Frustration is a reason to examine the issue, not proof that your interpretation is right.

## Give feedback in a relationship that can hold it

A manager cannot control whether feedback feels comfortable. You can control whether the conversation shows respect. Listen to the employee's view, separate intent from impact, and explain what needs to change.

Regular one on ones help because difficult feedback is less likely to be the first honest conversation between you. Use them to discuss work, expectations, and growth. Do not treat them as a substitute for addressing a time sensitive issue when it happens.

## Make the next step clear

Feedback should lead somewhere. Ask what the employee thinks happened. Agree on what they will try next time, and decide how you will follow up. If the issue involves a pattern, name the pattern and set a reasonable check in point.

The same care applies to positive feedback. "Good job" is pleasant, but it does not tell someone what to repeat. Point to the action and why it helped. For example, name how they clarified a decision before the group left a meeting.

If the employee sees the event differently, let them explain before you decide what to do. You might have missed a constraint or a decision they were following. Check the facts, then return to the impact and the expectation for next time. Feedback is a conversation, not a ruling.

## Do not save everything for the review

A performance review should summarize conversations the employee has already had with you. It should not be the first time they learn that a behavior is affecting their work or reputation. When feedback is new, timely, and specific, the employee has a fair chance to respond and improve.

Before you speak, ask yourself whether the goal is to help the person do better or to discharge your own irritation. If you are too angry to listen, take a short pause and return to the issue soon. Then have the conversation plainly.
