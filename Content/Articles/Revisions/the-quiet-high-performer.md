Proposed title: How to Support a Quiet High Performer on Your Team
Excerpt: A quiet employee may be engaged, overlooked, cautious, or simply less vocal. Ask what support and growth look like for that person instead of guessing from their communication style.

## Quiet does not tell you what someone needs

Some employees speak often in meetings. Others do careful work and say less. Neither pattern tells you whether a person is satisfied, ambitious, or planning to leave.

Managers can miss quieter employees because they do not create urgent problems. But treating silence as a warning sign can lead to a different mistake: assuming you know what the employee wants before asking. They may want a stretch assignment, a clearer path, more recognition, or simply room to keep doing strong work without being pushed into a different role.

The useful question is not "Which quiet person is about to quit?" It is "Have I asked what this person wants, and have I followed through on the answer?"

## Do not confuse low maintenance with no needs

A reliable employee may need little day-to-day help and still need feedback, recognition, resources, or a next challenge. If you only spend time with people who ask for it, the person who is least comfortable asking can become easy to overlook.

Use regular one-on-ones to make the conversation easier to start. Ask what part of the work has been most satisfying lately. Ask whether there is a project they would like to own or a skill they want to build. Ask what has been getting in their way. Then leave enough silence for a real answer.

Do not turn those questions into a script you rush through. The point is to learn what matters to this person. A short answer may mean they need time to think. Ask a follow-up, or return to it next week.

## Notice changes, then ask

A change in someone's usual pattern may be worth a conversation. Maybe they have stopped volunteering for work they used to enjoy, or their updates have become less detailed. That is a reason to check in, not proof that they are disengaged.

Use what you observed without telling them what it means. You might say, "You used to bring ideas to our project reviews, and I have noticed that less over the past few weeks. How are things going from your side?" Give them space to disagree with your read.

Avoid predictions such as "You have about a month before they leave." You cannot know that from a few signals, and acting as if you can may damage trust. Ask directly, listen, and respond to what the person actually says.

## Match growth to the person

Not every strong performer wants a promotion. Some want broader work, deeper expertise, a steadier schedule, or a chance to mentor someone. Ask what a good next six months would look like before deciding what opportunity to offer.

If the employee wants more scope, make the opportunity concrete. Explain what they would own, which decisions they would make, who they would work with, and how you will review the outcome. Do not describe extra work as development unless it gives the employee a real chance to learn or influence something.

Visibility can help when it supports the person's goals. Give them credit by name when discussing their work, and let them present it when they want that opportunity. Do not put someone on display as a substitute for a conversation about what they want.

## Follow through on what you hear

The conversation only matters if you do something with the answer. If the person asks for clearer priorities, agree on how you will set them. If they want to lead a project, identify a real project and talk through the support they will need. If they are content with their current work, respect that instead of inventing a growth problem.

Be honest about limits. You may not be able to offer a promotion or a project right away. Say what you can do, what you cannot promise, and when you will revisit the question. Then keep that date.

A useful follow-up is simple: "You said you want more experience presenting to leadership. I found a project update next month where you could present the results. Would that be useful to you?" The employee can accept, decline, or explain what would fit better.

## Put one question in your next one-on-one

Pick one question and ask it without an agenda hidden behind it: "What kind of work would you like more of?" or "Is there something you want me to notice that I might be missing?"

Listen for the answer. Write down what you agreed to do, then follow up when you said you would. That is how you make a quieter employee feel seen, if feeling seen is what they wanted. More importantly, it keeps you from managing a guess about their career instead of the person in front of you.
