# A Practical 30 Minute Structure for Manager One on Ones

A useful one on one gives an employee room to talk about what matters to them, where work is stuck, and what they want to learn. If your meetings turn into status reports, try separating those three topics from your own updates.

The Connect Framework is one way to organize a 30 minute meeting. It uses three ten minute sections: the person, their work, and their growth. Treat the timing as a starting point. Some weeks one topic will need more time than another.

## Start with the person

Open with a human question. How are things going? What has been taking their attention? Is anything outside work affecting their capacity? Let the employee decide how much to share. Personal questions should invite, not demand, disclosure.

This part is not small talk you have to get through before the "real" work. It helps you understand how the person is experiencing the week. Respect boundaries if they prefer to keep the conversation focused on work.

## Talk through current work

Ask what feels most pressing, where they are stuck, and what decision or resource would help. Your role is not to take over every problem. Clarify what they need from you, then remove a blocker when you can.

If a task or status update belongs in a shared project channel, move it there. A one on one is especially useful for context that benefits from a two way conversation: competing priorities, unclear ownership, or a concern the employee has not raised elsewhere.

## Make space for growth

Use the final part of the meeting to discuss learning and future work. What skill do they want to practice? What kind of assignment would stretch them? Is there feedback they need from you?

Do not promise a promotion or opportunity you cannot control. You can still look for a practical next step, such as letting them lead part of a project or arranging time with a colleague who knows the work they want to explore.

## Keep your agenda visible elsewhere

This structure does not mean a manager can never raise their own priorities. It means routine updates should not take over the only recurring conversation centered on one employee. Share operational updates in team meetings, writing, or a separate working session when that fits better.

Review the agenda together. If something urgent needs attention, handle it. If a topic needs more time, schedule it rather than rushing through the rest. The framework is useful only if it serves the conversation.

For example, if an employee brings a blocked project, spend more of the meeting clarifying what decision or help is needed. You can return to development next time and note that follow up. The three sections keep important topics visible, but they do not require equal time every week.

Try the structure for your next one on one. Ask what the employee wants to cover, leave room for blockers and development, and adjust the time to match what is actually going on.
